Title: The Future of Urban Transportation (Grade 12 Reading)

Instructions: Read the passage and answer the questions. Time limit: 15–20 minutes.

Passage:
Cities around the world are confronting a familiar dilemma: how to move more people, more quickly, while cutting pollution and congestion. In the past, expanding roads seemed like the obvious answer. Yet decades of evidence show that wider highways often invite more cars, a phenomenon known as “induced demand.” As lanes multiply, so do trips, until traffic slows to a crawl again. In response, city planners are shifting their attention from moving vehicles to moving people.

One promising direction is a networked approach that blends public transit, cycling, and pedestrian corridors with emerging technologies. Bus Rapid Transit (BRT) systems, for example, borrow the best features of subways—dedicated lanes, platform-level boarding, and frequent service—at a fraction of the cost. Meanwhile, protected bike lanes have demonstrated that when safety improves, ridership can surge across all age groups. These measures do more than add capacity; they reshape behavior by making efficient options convenient and reliable.

Technology will also play a growing role. Real-time data can synchronize traffic signals to reduce stop‑and‑go patterns that waste fuel. Demand‑responsive shuttles can extend the reach of transit into low‑density neighborhoods, while integrated payment apps can knit buses, trains, scooters, and car‑share into a single, user‑friendly experience. Crucially, however, innovation must be matched with policy. Without pricing mechanisms—such as congestion charges or market‑based parking fees—private cars will remain artificially cheap, and the incentives to switch modes will remain weak.

Ultimately, the most successful cities may be those that reimagine streets as public spaces rather than pipelines for cars. Wider sidewalks, urban trees, and traffic‑calmed intersections can improve air quality and public health, while also stimulating local commerce. The question is not whether people will move—that is inevitable in growing cities—but whether they can do so cleanly, safely, and with a sense of dignity. Transportation, in that sense, is not just an engineering challenge; it is a choice about what kind of city we want to build.

Questions:
